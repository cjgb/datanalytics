A theory-led qualitative essay arguing that Airbnb hosting lowers hosts' economic precarity while raising their 'vital' (existential) precarity, because hosts must turn themselves and their homes into enterprises.

Thesis (C1) Hosting on Airbnb reduces hosts' economic precariousness but increases their vital precariousness. partial

The qualitative descriptions of what hosts do (crafting profiles, managing and rationing sociability, being constantly available) are credible and well illustrated. The headline causal trade-off — less economic precarity, more vital precarity — is a theoretically motivated interpretation rather than a demonstrated finding: the economic half is not measured, the vital half rests on selected extreme cases, and reverse causality (precarious people host) is not addressed. Several quantitative-sounding assertions are unsourced.

Summary

Starting from Airbnb's marketing promise that anyone can earn extra income to recover purchasing power lost in the crisis, the article analyses the work performed by peer-to-peer hosts (explicitly excluding multi-listing professional hosts). Drawing on Foucault, Laval & Dardot and Brown, it frames the host as a neoliberal 'entrepreneur of the self' and, drawing on Postone, treats hosting activities as abstract labour that is organised over the reproductive sphere rather than over conventional work. It distinguishes material work (cleaning, key handover) from immaterial work (producing 'externalities', experiences and sociability) and illustrates the latter with excerpts from semi-structured interviews with 'dozens' of hosts in New York, San Francisco, Madrid and Barcelona. A final section, drawing on Lorey, Butler and Pérez Orozco, argues that the activity increases 'vital precariousness' (loss of control over home, time, intimacy) even as the income reduces economic precariousness; vivid vignettes (a host sleeping in a closet for 2.5 years, 'urban nomads' who vacate their homes at weekends) and an InsideAirbnb table of host response times are offered as evidence.

Research questions

  1. What form does the activity of Airbnb hosts take as work?
  2. How do hosts adopt an entrepreneurial logic to increase the profitability of hosting?
  3. What are the effects of hosting on hosts' economic and 'vital' precariousness?

Stated contribution

Applies theories of neoliberal subjectivation and precarity to platform-mediated home-sharing, proposing that the capital-labour conflict becomes a capital-life conflict for hosts.

How the argument is built

(1) Theory: the host as entrepreneurial subject (Foucault, Laval & Dardot, Brown). (2) Hosting as abstract labour built on the reproductive sphere (Postone; Gil 2018c 'prosumer labour'); hosts say it is work. (3) Typology of host work: material vs immaterial (externalities, sociability, reputation); illustrated with interview quotes. (4) Precarity as governmentality (Lorey, Butler); vignettes and response-time data show the invasion of life. (5) Conclusion: Airbnb reduces economic but increases vital precarity; precarity as a mode of being governed.

Evidence & data

Design

Qualitative. Theory-driven interpretive essay illustrated with excerpts from semi-structured interviews; one descriptive table from scraped platform data.

Scope: Peer-to-peer hosts (not multi-listing hosts) in four global cities, c. 2016-2018.

Methods

  • Interpretive reading of interview excerpts through a pre-specified theoretical lens
  • Descriptive tabulation of InsideAirbnb response-time categories

Transparency notes

No information on number of interviewees, interview period, sampling/recruitment, interview guide, coding or analysis procedure, or host characteristics (income, tenure, listing type). InsideAirbnb snapshot dates not given.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Footnote 1 restricts the analysis to hosts whose activity follows peer-to-peer principles, acknowledging that multi-listing hosts dominate the market.

Issues (9)

I1 major transparency Interview sample undocumentedpp. 3-4

The only information is 'decenas' of semi-structured interviews in four cities. Number of interviews, recruitment, dates, host profiles (single room vs whole home, income, tenure, ownership), interview guide and analytic procedure are not reported, so representativeness and analytic saturation cannot be judged.

Impact on conclusions: Limits all empirical claims to 'illustrative' status.

Affects: C1C10C15

I3 major causal inference Economic side of the thesis not measured; reverse causality unaddressedpp. 15-18

No income data are presented for the claimed reduction of economic precarity, and the vignettes suggest hosts were already precarious (hosting to pay rent or avoid eviction), so hardship may cause hosting rather than hosting causing hardship. No comparison with non-hosts or pre-hosting conditions.

Impact on conclusions: The causal 'trade-off' is plausible but not demonstrated.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: A theoretical-interpretive essay in critical social theory. The appropriate standard is not statistical representativeness but (a) conceptual coherence, (b) whether qualitative material genuinely illustrates the mechanisms proposed, and (c) whether conclusions are kept within what illustrative material can bear. Empirical quantifiers ('all', 'most', 'fastest-growing') are nonetheless held to an evidential standard.
