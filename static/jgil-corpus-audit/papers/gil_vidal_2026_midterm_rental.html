Using proprietary Idealista quarterly data (2019-2024) for Barcelona, Madrid and Málaga, the paper argues that mid-term rental (MTR) growth is driven mainly by regulatory evasion in Barcelona and by market dynamics in Madrid and Málaga, with transnational demand acting as an enabler rather than a driver.

Thesis (C1) MTR expansion responds to an interplay of regulatory incentives, market dynamics and transnational demand: regulatory avoidance is the primary driver in Barcelona, market dynamics and asset valorisation in Madrid and Málaga; transnational demand is an enabler rather than a driver. partial

Valuable first multi-city picture of Spain's MTR boom. The Barcelona descriptive evidence — MTR listings surging at each rent-control milestone while LTR listings collapse — makes regulatory avoidance a compelling explanation, and all reported figures reconcile. But the regression layer meant to adjudicate among hypotheses is not fit for purpose (24 trending observations, no regulation term, reverse causality in the rent-gap variable), so the city-specific attributions for Madrid and Málaga and the 'definitive' rejection of profit motives in Barcelona are not established.

Summary

Three hypotheses are posed: H1 regulatory blind spots (seasonal 1-11 month contracts are exempt from rent control and tenure rules), H2 market navigation (flexibility/liquidity in appreciating markets), H3 transnational demand (digital nomads, expats, students). Idealista data (24 quarters per city) provide listings by type, median asking rents, leads and the origin/language of searches. In Barcelona MTR listings reached 75.45% of residential listings by end-2024 (from 4.76% in 2019), jumping when rent controls were applied; residential listings fell 76.73%. Madrid's MTR ratio reached ~18%, Málaga's 11.57% (listings ×9). The MTR-LTR asking-rent premium ('rent gap') ended at 44.32% (Madrid), 16.71% (Barcelona) and 10.02% (Málaga). Simple and multiple OLS regressions on quarterly levels find: Barcelona — negative rent-gap coefficient (interpreted as regulatory evasion, H1); Málaga — rent gap, residential listings and price/m2 significant (H2); Madrid — price/m2 and listings significant (partial H2); foreign searches not significant in multivariate models (H3 as contextual enabler). Neighbourhood-level foreign-origin and foreign-language searches correlate at R2 > 0.91 and concentrate where MTRs are dense. Policy: extend regulation to seasonal contracts.

Research questions

  1. What drives the expansion of MTRs across Barcelona, Madrid and Málaga: regulatory blind spots, market navigation, or transnational demand?
  2. How are MTRs and international demand distributed within cities?

Stated contribution

First multi-city empirical analysis of mid-term rentals in Europe, comparing three explanatory mechanisms.

How the argument is built

Introduction with H1-H3 → literature on platform rentierism, regulatory blind spots, liquidity → policy context of the three cities → data/methods (Idealista, rent-gap proxy, regressions) → supply, price and rent-gap trends → demand (leads, foreign searches/language) → multiple regression → conclusions and limitations.

C3 weak mainempirical causal contestedpp. 9, 13

A declining rent gap alongside expanding MTRs in Barcelona (and a significant negative rent-gap coefficient) 'definitively rules out profit maximisation' and shows regulatory evasion is the dominant driver.

“definitively rules out profit maximisation as the primary driver”

Assessment: The negative association is open to other explanations: MTR supply growth itself lowers MTR asking prices, and shrinking LTR listings raise LTR asking prices (reverse causality/equilibrium), so the gap falls because MTRs expand. Moreover, under rent control the landlord's alternative is the capped legal rent, not the LTR asking price, so the asking-price gap does not measure the incentive. Evading controls is itself profit maximisation under a constraint; the dichotomy is false. 'Definitively' is not warranted from a single coefficient with N = 24.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Comparative descriptive time-series analysis of platform listings for three cities plus small-N OLS regressions (city-level quarterly levels; neighbourhood cross-sections).

Scope: Three Spanish cities, 2019-2024.

Methods

  • Descriptive trends (cumulative change, shares)
  • Rent-gap proxy: % difference in median asking €/m2 (MTR vs LTR)
  • Simple OLS of MTR listings on rent gap and on foreign searches (N = 23 per city)
  • Neighbourhood OLS of foreign-language on foreign-origin searches
  • Multiple OLS with four predictors (N = 24 per city)

Transparency notes

Rent-gap proxy limitations are candidly explained. Not reported: unit-root/autocorrelation diagnostics, whether variables are levels or changes (text says 'growth', models use levels), standard errors, and raw series. Two references are left as '[to be confirmed]'. Data are proprietary.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Idealista does not capture all listings; asking prices differ from contract rents.
  • Rent-gap proxy ignores vacancy, turnover and costs.
  • Small N (24) — 'results should be interpreted with caution'.
  • Regulatory effects only partially captured in the time window.
  • No data on tenant profiles.

Issues (6)

I1 major causal inference Time-series regressions on trending levels with N = 24pp. 4-5, 9, 13

OLS on quarterly levels of strongly trending series (listings, prices) without detrending, differencing, autocorrelation-robust errors or unit-root checks risks spurious significance; four correlated predictors (rent gap is a function of price/m2) with 24 observations; the text speaks of 'growth' while models use levels. The authors flag small N but not these issues.

Impact on conclusions: Regression results cannot discriminate among H1-H3 with confidence.

Affects: C1C4C5C6

I2 major causal inference H1 never directly testedpp. 9, 13

No regulation variable (dummies for Law 11/2020 in force, Law 12/2023, Catalan application in 2024) enters any model; H1 support is inferred from a negative rent-gap coefficient and descriptive timing. An interrupted time-series or difference-in-differences vs Madrid/Málaga would be the natural test.

Impact on conclusions: Barcelona's regulatory story rests on descriptive timing (which is actually quite persuasive) rather than the regression.

Affects: C1C3

I3 moderate logic Over-interpretation of the negative rent-gap coefficientpp. 9, 13

Reverse causality (MTR supply growth lowers MTR prices; LTR listing scarcity raises LTR asking prices) and mis-measured incentive (the capped legal rent, not asking LTR rent, is the landlord's alternative under control) can produce the negative sign. Evasion of controls is a form of profit maximisation, so 'rules out profit maximisation' is a false dichotomy.

Impact on conclusions: The H1 conclusion may be right, but not for the reason given.

Affects: C3

I4 moderate measurement Listings are flows, not stockspp. 5-6, 9

MTRs turn over (and are advertised) more often than LTRs; longer LTR leases and rent control reduce LTR vacancies reaching the platform; landlords may fill controlled units off-platform. Listing shares and leads-per-listing therefore exaggerate shifts in the underlying stock and in competition.

Impact on conclusions: Direction credible; magnitudes overstated.

Affects: C2C8

I5 minor arithmetic Small arithmetic/labelling slipspp. 5, 9, 13

Barcelona's rent gap fell 36.5 points from its Dec-2021 peak, or 17.2 points since 2019 — described as 'a compression of 17 percentage points in three years'. Madrid's 17.97% is the MTR/residential ratio, not 'of total listings' (≈15.2% of total). Simple-regression β (−0.94) and multiple-regression coefficient (−140.8) appear to be on different scales without explanation.

Impact on conclusions: Minor.

Affects: C3

I6 minor internal inconsistency Introduction vs results on H3; unfinished referencespp. 2, 10, 14-15

The introduction says transnational demand is not significant 'in any of the three cities', but simple regressions are significant in Madrid and Málaga (only multivariate models are null). Two references are printed with '[to be confirmed]' placeholders.

Impact on conclusions: Editorial; the conclusion itself is nuanced.

X1 contradiction moderate Did pandemic STRs return to the residential market?

Gil (2023) states that during the pandemic 'STRs did not return to the residential market (Gil et al., 2023)' and uses this to argue for housing-policy (not tourism-policy) responses. Gil, Vidal & Yrigoy (2026) cite the same source for STR units being 'temporarily redirected to residential or mid-term segments'. Sequera et al. (2022) document central Madrid long-term listings rising from 1,300 to 4,600 and back to 1,900, and Gil (2021) reported hosts 'directly ending the business and renting in the residential market'. The evidence supports a temporary switch, largely under seasonal contracts; the 2023 paper's unqualified statement understates it.

Implication: The policy argument in Gil (2023) should be qualified: switching occurred but was temporary and often outside long-term residential contracts.
