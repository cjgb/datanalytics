Summary

Written by two long-standing members and spokespeople of the Madrid and Barcelona tenants' unions, the paper introduces 'counter-hegemonic legislative strategies' to explain how unions secured post-neoliberal housing policy. Three mechanisms are proposed: (1) organised offensive tenant disobedience (Stay Put / partial rent strikes, Buildings in Struggle); (2) strategic use of social and mainstream media to shift common sense; (3) exploitation of institutional windows of opportunity by allying with minority coalition partners and left opposition and creating ungovernability. Four legislative episodes are narrated: defeat of the December 2018 LAU decree and approval of a stronger March 2019 version after the Argumosa 11 eviction; COVID-19 eviction bans following the 'Cancel Rent' campaign; the union-drafted Catalan rent control law (Llei 11/2020), later annulled by the Constitutional Court; and the 2023 Right to Housing Law. The authors argue that, though falling short of union demands, these outcomes disrupted the post-2008 accumulation fix, increased union power, and produced crises of representation within governing parties.

Research questions

  1. Under what conditions, and through which mechanisms, can tenants' movements push post-neoliberal housing policies?
  2. How do these struggles challenge neoliberal housing governance as a whole?

Stated contribution

The concept of counter-hegemonic legislative strategies and empirical evidence linking Spanish post-neoliberal housing policies to tenant struggles.

How the argument is built

Global context → literature on post-neoliberal policy and movement outcomes (Kadi et al., Byrne, Card, Mironova) → action-research method → concept and three mechanisms → four legislative episodes → discussion of three outcomes (partial policy shift, union power accumulation, crises of representation).

Evidence & data

Design

Qualitative. Activist/action research with participant observation by insiders, complemented by news and policy documents; narrative case study of legislative episodes.

Scope: Spain and Catalonia, 2018-2023.

Methods

  • Insider narrative process tracing of legislative episodes
  • Conceptual synthesis with social-movement outcome literature

Transparency notes

Positionality is disclosed clearly. No systematic data collection protocol, interviews with non-union actors, media content analysis, or explicit treatment of alternative explanations. Several claims attributed to anonymous ministers.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Legislative victories fell short of union demands.
  • Hard to estimate how many households actually joined the 2020 rent strike.
  • Other union activities not analysed for lack of space.

Issues (5)

I1 major missing counterevidence Insider evaluation without triangulationpp. 7, 17

Both authors are union members and spokespeople evaluating their own organisations' impact. Positionality is disclosed, but there is no reflexive treatment of bias, no interviews with legislators/ministries/landlord organisations, and key counterfactual claims rely on unnamed ministers.

Impact on conclusions: Causal attributions should be read as informed participant accounts.

Affects: C1C4C6

I2 major causal inference Alternative explanations for legislative outcomes not weighedpp. 14-17

Coalition agreement commitments, partisan competition, COVID-era policy diffusion (eviction moratoria internationally), budget bargaining and the votes of non-left parties are not systematically considered. The 2018 decree's defeat required a majority against, including parties outside the left bloc.

Impact on conclusions: Union contribution is plausible; 'key factor' status not established.

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.
