Using 55 interviews and a survey of 94 PAH groups, the paper argues that working-class migrant women — turned into subprime 'financial subjects' in the 2000s bubble — became key actors of the Spanish anti-eviction movement, whose struggles combined an 'immediate' and a 'wide' political temporality.

Thesis (C1) Working-class migrant women were key actors of the Spanish housing movement and their struggle combined immediate and wide political temporalities that reinforced each other. partial

A well-grounded qualitative account showing how PAH activism combined survival-oriented mutual aid with structural campaigns, and how women (many of them migrants) experienced empowerment through it. The 'double horizon' concept fits the material. Weaknesses are the superlative claim that migrant women were the hardest hit (no comparative data), the lack of any joint measure of migrant women in the survey, and an undocumented interview sample composition.

Summary

Adopting social reproduction theory (rather than 'care') as a frame, the authors trace how migrants were drawn into mortgage debt through targeted marketing, informal 'hooks' and cross-guarantees, and then hit first by unemployment and foreclosure. Migrant women, stereotyped as 'good payers' and often left as sole breadwinners after 'male-parent flight', became central in the PAH. The 'double horizon of political temporality' concept distinguishes immediate outcomes (stopping evictions, social rents, mutual aid across social reproduction, empowerment: self-esteem, expertise, collective reframing and escape from patriarchal subordination) from wide outcomes (bank negotiation protocols, legislative proposals, municipal declarations, public opinion). Survey results: in about one third of PAH groups migrants were the majority, 84% of evictees were women, 97% of groups joined demonstrations, 84% bank occupations, 48% drafted regional laws, 58% won municipal support. The two temporalities are argued to reinforce each other, with wide mobilisation a necessary condition for immediate success.

Research questions

  1. How did working-class migrant women become key actors of housing activism in post-2008 Spain?
  2. What social and political outcomes did their struggles produce, and how do immediate and long-term goals relate?

Stated contribution

An analytical framework integrating intersectional structures, spatio-temporal social reproduction and financialisation; the concept of a 'double horizon of political temporality'.

How the argument is built

Financialisation and social reproduction → critique of 'care' framing → from intersectionality to dual temporality → migrants as financial subjects (interviews) → immediate outcomes and empowerment → wide outcomes (survey + interviews) → conclusions.

C1 partial thesisinterpretive non trivialpp. 1-3, 18-19

Working-class migrant women were key actors of the Spanish housing movement and their struggle combined immediate and wide political temporalities that reinforced each other.

Assessment: Interviews richly document both temporalities among PAH women, and survey data show a majority-female, heavily migrant and strongly mobilised base. But the survey never measures migrant women jointly (gender and origin are reported separately, as group-level estimates), so the specific centrality of migrant women is inferred, and the reinforcement mechanism is illustrated rather than tested.

Design

Mixed. Qualitative interview study with a descriptive organisational survey; engaged/activist positionality.

Methods

Transparency notes

Interview totals and period given, plus positionality; missing: interviewee breakdown by gender/origin/role, coding scheme, survey instrument and item-level n.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Authors were activists in other housing collectives (not PAH); survey co-designed with activists.
  • Race and racialisation deserve more attention.

Issues (4)

I1 moderate overgeneralization Superlative comparative claims without comparative datapp. 1, 10, 18

'Hardest hit and most exploited' and 'more prone/more frequently targeted' are not supported by any comparison of outcomes across groups; the study's data are about PAH participants only.

Impact on conclusions: Plausible claims presented as findings.

Affects: C2C4

I2 moderate measurement Migrant × gender composition not measuredp. 10

Survey items report gender and migrant shares separately and as estimates by group representatives; the intersection 'migrant women' — the paper's central subject — is never quantified.

Impact on conclusions: Centrality of migrant women (vs women or migrants generally) rests on qualitative material.

Affects: C1C5

I3 moderate transparency Interview sample not describedp. 3

55 interviews over seven years in nine cities, but no breakdown by gender, origin, role or tenure; only ~7 interviewees are quoted; no indication of how prevalent each theme was.

Impact on conclusions: Limits assessment of representativeness of the empowerment narratives.

Affects: C1C4C6C7

I4 minor causal inference Necessary-condition and cross-movement claims untestedpp. 16, 18-19

The claim that wide mobilisation is necessary for immediate success, and that other movements lack immediate goals, are not examined against contrasting cases.

Impact on conclusions: Interpretive.
