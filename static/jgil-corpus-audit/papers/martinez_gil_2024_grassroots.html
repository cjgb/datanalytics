Compares campaigns against Bankia (2008-13 austerity/bailout period) and Blackstone (2013-20 state-led reignition period) to argue that each phase of housing financialization shaped a distinct form of grassroots response, and that such campaigns can successfully challenge financial corporations.

Thesis (C1) Each type of grassroots response was shaped by a distinct process of capital accumulation through housing financialization: austerity/bailouts (2008-13) produced anti-eviction and accountability campaigns against Bankia; state-led reignition (2013-20) produced tenant campaigns against Blackstone; protest dynamics are 'essentially defined' by political-economy cycles. partial

A rich narrative of two emblematic Spanish anti-financialization campaigns that convincingly shows grassroots actors won concrete concessions from powerful financial firms. The central comparative claim — that each financialization phase shaped its movement's form — is plausible but rests on two purposively selected cases, and claims about reputational damage and halting speculative strategies are not measured. A few factual slips (€4bn black cards) and cross-paper inconsistencies appear.

Summary

Based on 35 activist interviews (2011-2020), participant observation, media and legal documents, the paper distinguishes two periods of Spanish housing financialization: 2008-13 (bank bailouts, sanitization, SAREB, austerity) epitomised by Bankia (€22.4bn bailout, leading evictor), and post-2013 (SOCIMI and LAU reforms, privatizations, global corporate landlords) epitomised by Blackstone. Anti-Bankia activism combined PAH eviction blockades and negotiations (leading to bank protocols and social rents), consumer litigation over preferential shares (ADICAE), the crowdfunded 15MpaRato lawsuit (Rato convicted), and squatting of bank properties and offices. Anti-Blackstone activism included Raval vs Blackstone (festival, coalition, deal regularising six of nine flats) and Madrid vs Blackstone (bloques en lucha, collective bargaining demands, UN rapporteurs, lawsuit). The authors conclude that protest dynamics, opponents and claims are 'essentially defined by the political economy conditions of every historical cycle', while activists learned from prior mobilisations, and that campaigns undermined both firms' reputations.

Research questions

  1. How did housing campaigns against financial corporations unfold in Spain?
  2. To what extent were they successful?
  3. Why did they occur in these specific ways?

Stated contribution

Focus on financial firms as direct opponents and on structural periods of financialization as explanatory of movement forms.

How the argument is built

Social movements vs corporations (King; Balsiger) → periodisation of Spanish financialization → Bankia campaigns → Blackstone campaigns (Raval, Madrid) → discussion: structural cycles shape movements; commonalities.

Each type of grassroots response was shaped by a distinct process of capital accumulation through housing financialization: austerity/bailouts (2008-13) produced anti-eviction and accountability campaigns against Bankia; state-led reignition (2013-20) produced tenant campaigns against Blackstone; protest dynamics are 'essentially defined' by political-economy cycles.

Assessment: The correspondence between periods, dominant grievances (mortgage evictions vs rent hikes) and targets (bank vs landlord fund) is well described and intuitive. But with two cases, each chosen as the firm that 'best illustrates' a period, the design cannot separate structural determination from organisational learning, political opportunities (new left municipal governments from 2015) or tenure shifts; the authors themselves note overlaps (PAH active after 2013) and 'not mechanistic' learning, which sits uneasily with 'essentially defined'.

Issues (4)

I1 moderate sampling Two cases selected to fit the periodisationpp. 4-7, 16-17

Bankia and Blackstone are chosen as the firms that 'best illustrate' each period; with N = 2 and no within-period variation or counter-cases, the claim that political-economy cycles 'essentially define' protest dynamics cannot be distinguished from learning, opportunity structures or tenure composition.

Impact on conclusions: Periodisation is a plausible heuristic, not a demonstrated determinant.

Affects: C1C8

I2 moderate causal inference Outcomes attributed without counterfactual or measurement

Bank protocols, reputational damage and 'halting' Blackstone are attributed to campaigns based on activist testimony and press; rival explanations (regulators, courts, bailout conditions, market cycle) and magnitudes are not assessed; the proposed media-based reputation indicator is not operationalised.

Impact on conclusions: Documented local wins; broader effects uncertain.

Links to the rest of the corpus

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.

Martínez & Gil (2022) say the ~5,000 units were sold 'below market prices, and even below construction costs'. Implication: Below-market pricing rests on court findings, not on the cost table.
