Summary

The essay traces the rise of GCLs (private equity, REITs, asset managers) through pre-2008 privatisations (Berlin GSW, New York rent-stabilised stock) and post-2008 conditions: quantitative easing and low rates, Basel III, devalued distressed assets, and a shift from banks to alternative asset managers (Financialisation 1.0 vs 2.0). Short-termist fund structures drive rent increases, renovations, tenure conversion and maintenance cuts. In response, a new generation of tenant unions deploys anti-eviction blockades, rent strikes, multi-building and cross-border organising (upward scale shift), reputational and legal campaigns, and co-drafted legislation. Policy shifts include Denmark's 'Blackstone intervention', Spain's 2019 lease extensions and 2023 rent controls, Los Angeles/San Francisco anti-harassment and right-to-organise ordinances, large-landlord obligations in Catalonia, and Berlin's socialisation referendum — many of them curtailed by courts or enforcement problems. The authors note the Global North bias of the issue and conclude that outcomes are uncertain but radical tenant practices are beginning to challenge housing financialisation.

Research questions

  1. How did GCLs rise and what are their operational logics?
  2. What repertoires and demands characterise the new cycle of tenant contention against GCLs?

Stated contribution

A preliminary comparative synthesis framing the special issue's case studies (Spain, Germany, UK, US) within a common cycle of contention.

How the argument is built

Rise of GCLs (macro-financial drivers) → operational logics and aggressive practices → new cycle of contention (repertoires, scale shift) → legal and policy change (from post-neoliberalisation to socialisation) → structure of the issue.

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.
