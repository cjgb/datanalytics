Argues that three coordinated 2013 state actions — SOCIMI (REIT) reform, rental-law (LAU) deregulation and the sale of Madrid social housing to Blackstone and Goldman Sachs — formed a 'policy package' that reignited housing financialization in Spain.

Thesis (C1) Spanish authorities strategically recreated a finance-friendly environment through a coordinated state-led policy package (SOCIMI reform, LAU reform, social-housing privatizations) that reignited housing financialization after 2013. partial

An informative account of the 2012-2013 SOCIMI and LAU reforms and the Madrid social-housing sales, which credibly shows that Spanish policy made housing markedly more attractive to global funds. The core thesis — that these measures were a coordinated package that caused the 2014-2020 financialization boom — is an interpretation based on timing; confounding macro drivers acknowledged by the authors are not weighed, and the privatization table contains arithmetic errors and does not show below-market prices.

Summary

After 2008, Spanish banks held devalued real-estate assets and NPLs and faced Basel III. The authors argue that global investment funds were the only actors able to absorb them, and that the state strategically recreated a finance-friendly environment through a 'policy package': (1) the 2012/13 SOCIMI reform (0% corporate tax, 3-year holding period, lower capital and asset minimums, no leverage cap); (2) the 2013 LAU reform (minimum lease 5→3 years, waivable right of first refusal, free rent updates); (3) sales of ~4,800 social rental homes by Madrid's EMVS (1,861 to Blackstone) and IVIMA (2,935 to Goldman Sachs/Azora) at below-market prices, later condemned by courts. The package was implemented rapidly by the Partido Popular across government levels with poor democratic accountability. It is credited with the 2014-2020 boom: 73 SOCIMIs by 2019 (€23.5bn capitalisation), mass fund acquisitions of homes and NPLs (NPLs −70% 2013-2019), house prices +31% and rents +50%. Evidence comes from legislation, court rulings, official statistics, company data and news media.

Research questions

  1. How did state actions after 2008 reignite housing financialization in Spain?
  2. How did different policy instruments and levels of government operate jointly as a 'policy package'?

Stated contribution

The concept of a state-led 'policy package' coordinating financial, rental-law and privatization measures across scales to create a spatial fix.

How the argument is built

Context (bubble, crash, Basel III, GCLs) → literature on state-led financialization and regulated deregulation → SOCIMI reform → LAU reform → social housing privatizations → timing/coordination → post-2013 outcomes (SOCIMIs, fund purchases, NPLs, prices) → conclusions.

C1 partial thesisempirical causal contestedpp. 1-3, 14-16

Spanish authorities strategically recreated a finance-friendly environment through a coordinated state-led policy package (SOCIMI reform, LAU reform, social-housing privatizations) that reignited housing financialization after 2013.

Assessment: That the three measures were adopted in 2012-2013 and each made Spanish housing more attractive to investors is well documented. That they were 'strategically coordinated' as a package is inferred from timing and single-party control; no documentary evidence of coordination is presented. That they caused the 2014-2020 boom is asserted from before/after sequence alone, while the authors themselves list powerful concurrent drivers (ECB QE, very low interest rates, the 'wall of money', SAREB, bank restructuring) and mention that REITs were introduced elsewhere (e.g. Ireland) at the same time.

Issues (5)

I1 major causal inference Post hoc causal attribution of the 2014-2020 boompp. 11-14

The boom is attributed to the package because it followed it. Concurrent drivers acknowledged elsewhere in the paper (QE, record-low rates, global liquidity, SAREB, bank restructuring, economic recovery) are not weighed, and no comparison case is used (e.g. countries adopting REIT regimes without LAU changes or privatizations).

Impact on conclusions: The package plausibly contributed; its decisive role is not demonstrated.

Affects: C1C7

I2 moderate causal inference Coordination and intent inferred from timingpp. 8-12

Strategic coordination and design intent are asserted ('wisely coordinate', 'designed to') on the basis of co-occurrence and single-party control, without primary documentary evidence; the stated official rationales are dismissed rather than assessed.

Impact on conclusions: The 'package' is an interpretive construct more than a documented plan.

Affects: C1C4C6

I3 moderate arithmetic Table 2 errors and mis-described evidencep. 10, Table 2

(a) Row differences sum to €17,654,238 (= 200,726,431 − 183,072,193), not the €17,799,057 reported; per-home total is €6,015, not €5,394. Impact on conclusions: Below-market claim rests on court findings, not on this table.

X2 numeric inconsistency minor Scale of evictions after 2008

Martínez & Gil (2021, films) report 'on average 80,000 evictions per year between 2009 and 2015' (≈560,000), while Gil & Martínez (2021, state-led) report 409,000 evictions for 2009-2018. The films paper's own footnote gives repossessions of 40,740 (2011) and 64,902 (2015), ~80% homes.

Implication: Background figures need harmonised definitions (foreclosures vs evictions vs repossessions).

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.

Martínez & Gil (2022) say the ~5,000 units were sold 'below market prices, and even below construction costs'. The IVIMA table in Gil & Martínez (2021) shows the 2,935-unit sale exceeded construction cost by ≈€17.7m overall (below cost in only 3 of 11 municipalities), and its totals row is mis-summed.
