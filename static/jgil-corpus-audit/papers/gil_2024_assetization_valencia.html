Summary

The paper argues that STR-led urban change is best understood not as gentrification (no upper-class replacement) nor touristification (not the traditional tourism industry) but as a specific form of housing assetization: STRs raise exchange value, yield and liquidity of housing (escaping tenancy law), attracting capital. Three elements define the strategy: STR-induced rent gaps, emergence of professional intermediaries, and profit-maximising practices (tourist areas, small flats, high guest density). Displacement is decomposed into direct, exclusionary and pressure forms, with STR displacement said to be primarily financial and to target tenants, implying class antagonism. Empirically it re-uses the 31 July 2019 Valencia InsideAirbnb scrape (7,203 listings) with AirDNA occupancy, Idealista rents and INE stock: rent gap €497-958/month on average; top-gap districts €10.8-11.5k/year; tourist districts generate 51.8% of STR revenue; multi-listing hosts 58.54% of listings; guests per bedroom higher for multi-hosts and small flats; top-10% earners get 52.79% of revenue. It estimates 3,663 homes extracted and ~9,047 people directly displaced, rental supply in the two tourist districts reduced by ≥27.89% and ≥14.09%, and argues that STR harms should be addressed by housing rather than tourism policy.

Research questions

  1. Is 'STR housing assetization' a more suitable concept than gentrification/touristification for STR-driven change?
  2. Can the accumulation strategy be observed empirically in the STR market of a city (Valencia)?

Stated contribution

A new concept (STR housing assetization) with a three-element mechanism and an STR-specific typology of displacement, plus an empirical illustration.

How the argument is built

Literature: financialization/assetization → STRs raise rent and liquidity → professionalisation → touristification → displacement typology and class analysis. Methods: Valencia 2019 data. Analysis: rent gap; professional hosts; densities; revenue concentration; displacement estimates. Discussion: concept, class antagonism, housing-policy implications.

C1 partial thesistheoretical contestedpp. 1-2, 15

STR-driven urban change is best conceptualised as 'STR housing assetization' — a distinct urban accumulation-by-dispossession strategy — rather than as gentrification, touristification or tourism gentrification; this term has greater explanatory power.

Assessment: The conceptual argument is coherent and well grounded in the financialization literature, and the distinctions drawn (who is displaced; role of tenancy law) are genuinely useful. But 'greater explanatory power' is never tested: the Valencia evidence (rent gaps, professionalisation, concentration) would be equally consistent with the rival framings, and the asset-specific features (liquidity, capital flows into housing, investor ownership) are not measured.

C2 partial maintheoretical common knowledgepp. 3-4

STRs increase housing's rent yield (rent gap) and its flexibility/liquidity as an asset because they escape tenancy law, making STRs a mechanism of housing financialization.

Assessment: The yield side is supported by the rent-gap estimate; the liquidity/flexibility side is argued from literature (Kemp; Dagkouli-Kyriakoglou et al.) with no Valencia evidence (e.g. sales, holding periods, investor purchases).

C3 partial mainempirical comparative common knowledgepp. 9-11

Converting a dwelling to STR in Valencia yields on average €497-958/month more than residential letting (over €10.7k/year in the top four districts); the rent gap remains high in tourist districts despite high supply, because tourist demand is strong.

Assessment: A positive gap is plausible. Magnitude depends on un-tested assumptions (70 m2, asking rents, list prices, uniform district occupancy). The method differs from Gil (2021) on the identical dataset (which reported €500-1,200) without explanation. The demand-based explanation for persistent gaps is conjecture from a single cross-section.

STR markets are controlled by a small number of professional actors: multi-listing hosts are 28.34% of hosts but hold 58.54% of listings; 15 hosts hold 660 listings.

Assessment: Counts reconcile (see Gil 2021 audit); the 71.91% single-host share is a carried-over error (should be 71.66%).

C6 partial mainempirical comparative non trivialpp. 11-12

Professional hosts raise yields through higher guest densities: more guests per bedroom among multi-listing and high-availability hosts and in small flats; small flats are over-represented among multi-hosts; this creates displacement pressure and 'low-cost' tourism.

Assessment: The descriptive differences are reported (2.05 vs 2.36 guests/bedroom), but they measure maximum permitted guests, not actual occupants; differences are modest; the link to displacement pressure is asserted from literature. The paper equates this density with 'occupancy rate', the same term used for booked-night occupancy, creating ambiguity. 'Yield' is not computed per host type.

C8 weak mainempirical causal non trivialpp. 13, 16

About 3,663 homes were extracted from the rental market and ~9,047 people directly displaced by STRs in Valencia over the last decade; in the discussion this becomes 'a minimum of 9,047 residents'.

“It was estimated that a minimum of 9,047 residents have suffered this form of displacement in Valencia.”

Assessment: Arithmetic is correct (3,663 x 2.47 = 9,048) but the estimate assumes every entire home listed >60 days previously housed a tenant household of average size that was displaced. Former owner-occupied, second, vacant, newly built or purpose-built tourist units would all inflate the count, while the snapshot cannot date conversions to 'the last decade'. The figure is an upper-bound scenario, yet the discussion calls it a minimum.

Evidence: E8 · Issues: I6

C9 weak mainempirical causal non trivialpp. 13-14, 16

In the two tourist districts STRs equal 39.6% and 27.46% of rental stock and reduced residential rental supply by at least 27.89% and 14.09%; together with 54-58% rent rises (2013-19) this shows STRs reduce supply and increase rents, causing exclusionary displacement.

Assessment: Same stock-ratio logic as Gil (2021) plus an assumption that multi-host listings were 'highly probably' withdrawn from residential rental — presented as a lower bound. Rent rises are co-temporal but not attributed: Ciutat Vella's increase was only the fourth highest, so districts with far fewer STRs rose faster, which weakens the causal reading the paper then states ('As a result, STRs both reduce ... and increase ...').

STRs generate displacement pressure only where traditional tourism already does (Ciutat Vella: capacity 22.77% of residents); elsewhere they do not create coexistence problems or deteriorate livability.

Assessment: Inferred from capacity ratios alone; no data on noise, complaints, retail change or resident perceptions. The negative claim ('do not create coexistence problems') is as unsupported as a positive one would be, and sits in tension with the paper's own earlier statement that STRs deteriorate community life within buildings (a building-level effect that does not require area-level concentration).

Evidence & data

Design

Mixed. Conceptual argument followed by a single-city, single-snapshot descriptive quantitative illustration (same dataset as Gil 2021, Cuadernos Geográficos).

Scope: Valencia, 2019 (with 2013-2019 rent changes).

Methods

  • Conceptual synthesis
  • Descriptive shares and district indicators
  • Rent-gap estimate (price x district occupancy − 15% commission − 20% management vs 70 m2 x rent/m2)
  • Pearson correlations of district distributions by host band
  • Back-of-envelope displacement estimate (listings >60 days x household size)

Transparency notes

Assumptions stated; rent-gap method differs from Gil (2021) on the same data without comment; correlation inputs (district vectors) not reported; no significance tests shown though 'significant' is claimed.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • 'It is impossible to determine the number of direct displacements' — the figure is an estimate.
  • Rent increases 'cannot be attributed exclusively to STRs'.
  • Pre-pandemic scrape chosen deliberately.

Issues (9)

I1 major conceptual Concept's distinctive features not operationalised

What distinguishes 'assetization' from rent-gap-driven touristification (liquidity, capital switching, investor ownership, asset-class treatment) is not measured: the data are listing-level only, with no ownership, transaction or capital-flow information. Hence the empirical section cannot adjudicate between the proposed concept and rival framings, and 'greater explanatory power' is asserted rather than demonstrated.

Impact on conclusions: The concept remains a plausible theoretical proposal; the Valencia case illustrates but does not test it.

Affects: C1C2

I6 major overgeneralization Displacement estimate presented as a minimumpp. 13, 16

The 9,047 figure assumes every high-availability entire home displaced a tenant household; it is explicitly an estimate on p. 13 ('are believed to', 'are thought to') but becomes 'a minimum of 9,047 residents' in the discussion. The same 'at least' framing is applied to the supply-reduction figures, which rest on the assumption that multi-host listings were withdrawn from residential rental.

Impact on conclusions: Headline displacement numbers should be read as upper-bound scenarios.

Affects: C8C9C13

I8 major causal inference Causal claims about rents and livability from cross-sectional ratiospp. 13-16

Rent increases are co-temporal only (and Ciutat Vella ranked 4th), and livability effects are inferred from capacity ratios; both are then stated as outcomes ('As a result, STRs ... increase residential rental prices'; 'does not deteriorate community livability').

Impact on conclusions: These conclusions are hypotheses consistent with the data.

Affects: C9C10

I2 moderate transparency Rent-gap method changed from earlier paper on identical datap. 9

Gil (2021) used price x available days x occupancy − 20% and found €500-1,200/month; this paper uses price x occupancy − 15% commission − 20% and finds €497-958/month, without noting the change or reconciling results. Key assumptions (70 m2, asking rents, list prices) are unchanged and untested.

Impact on conclusions: Magnitude uncertain; a positive gap likely.

Affects: C3

I5 moderate measurement Guest capacity treated as actual occupancypp. 11-12

'Occupancy rate' is used both for booked-night share (AirDNA) and for maximum guests per bedroom; the latter is compared with average household size (2.47) as if it were actual occupants. Also '40% / 30% / 20%' of listings by guests per bedroom sum to 90%.

Impact on conclusions: Density differences are suggestive only.

Affects: C6

I3 minor arithmetic Carried-over data errorspp. 9-11, 13

Sample arithmetic (7,654 − 451 − 46 ≠ 7,203) and the 71.91% single-host share (should be 71.66%) are repeated from Gil (2021). Also 3,663 homes vs 74.75% x 4,920 = 3,678.

Impact on conclusions: None on conclusions.

Affects: C4

I4 minor measurement Part-whole correlations and unreported significancep. 11

Band distributions are correlated with an overall distribution that includes them; no n, test or p-values are given; the pattern is not monotonic.

Impact on conclusions: Weakens a secondary claim; the Ciutat Vella overrepresentation may still hold.

Affects: C5

I7 minor measurement Modelled revenue and 'impact' language

Revenue and 'yield' are modelled from district occupancy and list price; impact on the residential market is inferred, not measured. Over-representation units (percent vs points) are ambiguous.

Impact on conclusions: Minor.

Affects: C3C7

I9 minor logic Overstated policy claimp. 17

'It is therefore impossible for STR-induced rent gaps to emerge under a strict housing regulation' is an unqualified deduction; the cited pandemic evidence is in tension with other corpus papers that report some STR-to-residential switching.

Impact on conclusions: Recommendation direction reasonable; certainty overstated.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: A conceptual paper with an empirical illustration. The concept should be judged on coherence, distinctiveness and usefulness; the empirical section on whether it actually shows the mechanism specific to the concept, and whether estimates are framed with appropriate uncertainty.

X1 contradiction moderate Did pandemic STRs return to the residential market?

Gil (2023) states that during the pandemic 'STRs did not return to the residential market (Gil et al., 2023)' and uses this to argue for housing-policy (not tourism-policy) responses. Gil, Vidal & Yrigoy (2026) cite the same source for STR units being 'temporarily redirected to residential or mid-term segments'. Sequera et al. (2022) document central Madrid long-term listings rising from 1,300 to 4,600 and back to 1,900, and Gil (2021) reported hosts 'directly ending the business and renting in the residential market'. The evidence supports a temporary switch, largely under seasonal contracts; the 2023 paper's unqualified statement understates it.

Implication: The policy argument in Gil (2023) should be qualified: switching occurred but was temporary and often outside long-term residential contracts.

X4 data reuse moderate Same Valencia 2019 scrape, undisclosed change of rent-gap method

Gil (2021) and Gil (2023) analyse the identical 31 July 2019 InsideAirbnb Valencia dataset. Errors propagate (7,654 − 451 − 46 ≠ 7,203; single-host share 71.91% instead of 71.66%). The rent-gap formula changes (availability-based vs occupancy with 15% commission) giving €500-1,200 vs €497-958 per month, without acknowledgement; the 'not habitual residence' threshold shifts from 90/182 days to 60 days.

Implication: Readers cannot tell which estimate is preferred; magnitudes are assumption-driven.

X5 method drift moderate Shifting thresholds for 'commercial' / 'non-residential' listings

Availability >182 days defines commercial listings in Gil (2021) and Gil & Sequera (2020); Gil (2021) also uses >90 days to argue non-residence; Gil (2023) uses >60 days to estimate homes 'extracted from the rental market' and displacement.

Implication: Lower thresholds mechanically raise displacement estimates; sensitivity analysis is never reported.

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.
