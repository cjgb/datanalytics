Summary

The paper frames 2015-2023 pro-tenant measures (Catalan Laws 24/2015, 11/2020 and extensions; RDL 7/2019, RDL 11/2020; Law 12/2023) as 'post-neoliberal' and analyses 7 legal preambles, 8 parliamentary debate transcripts and 16 landlord-organisation/think-tank documents. Three recurring arguments are identified (Table 1 maps them to actors and laws). (1) Vulnerable landlord: rebutted with LCS 2021 data — landlords ~9% of households, mean disposable income €46,725 (median €40,293) vs tenants €22,183 (€18,457); only 6.5% of landlord households at risk of poverty (0.6% of all households) vs 36.3% of tenants; simulations of rent cuts barely change this; institutional landlords are not funded by ordinary Spanish savers (pension funds 0.14% in real estate). (2) Counterproductive effects: challenged by land-rent/monopoly theory, the 'speed limit' on supply, small build-to-rent output, and a mixed empirical literature on rent control. (3) Property rights: rebutted via social constitutionalism (Spanish Constitution arts. 33, 47, 128) and historical housing rationing (36 of 47 jurisdictions). The authors sketch a counter-discourse centred on renters.

Research questions

  1. What are the main arguments used by dominant actors to limit or contest pro-tenant measures in Catalonia and Spain?
  2. How well do these arguments hold up theoretically and empirically?

Stated contribution

Identification of the three most forceful anti-tenant arguments and their critical appraisal; outline of a counter-discourse grounded in land-rent theory and social constitutionalism.

How the argument is built

Generation rent and post-neoliberalisation → political economy context and CDA method (Tables 1-2) → vulnerable landlord (data) → counterproductive effects (theory + evidence review) → property rights (legal/normative) → conclusions and counter-discourse.

C6 partial maintheoretical contestedpp. 10-11

The counterproductive-effects argument rests on a competitive-market assumption that ignores the monopolistic nature of land ownership; landlords can charge 'whatever the demand will bear', leaving room to control rents without preventing cost coverage.

Assessment: Land-rent theory is a legitimate counter-frame and the 'speed limit' literature is apt. But 'charge whatever demand will bear' overstates landlords' market power in a market with many competing owners, and monopoly rent in land does not by itself show that controls leave supply and maintenance incentives unaffected.

Evidence & data

Design

Mixed. Critical discourse analysis of a defined document corpus, followed by theoretical and empirical appraisal of each argument using survey microdata and literature.

Scope: Catalonia and Spain, 2015-2023.

Methods

  • Deductive-inductive thematic coding of discourse
  • Descriptive statistics and static poverty simulations
  • Literature review
  • Legal-normative argument

Transparency notes

Corpus composition and coding procedure described; selection criteria for transcripts reasonably explained. LCS landlord definition and weighting not detailed; no inter-coder reliability.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Paucity of data on PRS ownership structure.
  • The paper does not analyse discourse circulation in depth, focusing on validity of claims.
  • Rent-control evidence is mixed; impacts should be evaluated case by case.

Issues (4)

I1 moderate logic Low baseline supply does not refute supply effects of regulationpp. 11-12

Arguing that the counterproductive claim 'loses force' because the unregulated market supplies little affordable housing conflates the level of supply with the marginal effect of regulation on it.

Impact on conclusions: Weakens one strand of the rebuttal; the case-by-case conclusion remains reasonable.

I2 minor missing counterevidence Asymmetric scrutinypp. 10-12

Opponents' arguments are tested rigorously while evidence unfavourable to the authors' position (e.g. quality deterioration found by most studies per Kholodilin) is countered with an older critique; the monopoly-rent claim is asserted strongly.

Impact on conclusions: Tone of advocacy; conclusions still broadly defensible.

X10 citation chain moderate The 'policy package' causal claim becomes a premise

Gil & Martínez (2021) attribute the 2014-2020 boom to the 2013 SOCIMI/LAU/privatization package, post hoc. Later papers state as settled that 'the reforms achieved their objective' (Gil & Palomera 2024), that the package 'contributed to generating expectations of profitability' (Vidal et al. 2024), and build periodisations on it (Martínez & Gil 2022; Gil 2023). The 2025 special-issue introduction instead foregrounds QE, low rates and Basel III as drivers of GCL expansion.

Implication: A contested interpretation gains apparent robustness through self-citation; the programme's own later synthesis implicitly weakens it.
