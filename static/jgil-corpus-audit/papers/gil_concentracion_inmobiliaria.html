Government-commissioned report arguing, from Banco de España EFF microdata (2008-2022) and Catastro statistics (2008-2025), that Spain's housing problem is one of increasingly concentrated property ownership and polarisation between non-owners and multi-owners.

Thesis (C1) Spain's housing problem is to a large extent a problem of the distribution of property: ownership is concentrating and households are polarising into non-owners and multi-owners, consolidating a dual society. partial

The direction — more renting, more landlord households, more households with several properties, fewer with exactly one — is credibly documented and arithmetically consistent. But the headline numbers are framed more strongly than the data allow: 'property' includes garages and commercial premises, the 'owner' category excludes owner-occupying landlords (so owner-occupation did not fall to 63.9%), the multi-owner 'majority' holds only in Catastro, and category growth is read as household-level accumulation.

Summary

The report documents (i) a tenure shift 2008-2022: 'owner' households fall from 79% to 63.9%, tenants rise from 11.9% to 19.2%, 'landlord' households from 3.4% to 9.8%; (ii) in Catastro, the share of holders with 2+ urban properties rises from 46.1% to 51.7% (2008-2025), and counts of holders grow faster the larger the portfolio band (−3.7% for single-property holders up to +51.6% for 6-10); (iii) in EFF, properties held by households with >10 properties rise from 138,000 to 626,000, and households with 0 properties rise 63% while those with 2+ rise 54% and those with 1 fall 22%. It concludes that the housing crisis is a crisis of patrimonial inequality, that two million new properties have not widened access but reinforced accumulation, and that Spain is becoming a dual society. A methodological note states that EFF 'inmuebles' include garages, warehouses, shops, offices and hotels (plots excluded).

Research questions

  1. How has the distribution of property ownership among Spanish households changed since 2008?
  2. Is property increasingly concentrated, and are households being polarised between non-owners and multi-owners?

Stated contribution

Descriptive evidence for policy: reframing the housing problem as one of wealth distribution rather than only supply or prices.

How the argument is built

Section 1: tenure change (EFF). Section 2: multi-ownership and portfolio-band growth (Catastro) and property volumes by band (EFF). Section 3: polarisation of households (EFF). Conclusions. Methodological note (definitions, sources).

C1 partial thesisinterpretive contestedpp. 4, 14

Spain's housing problem is to a large extent a problem of the distribution of property: ownership is concentrating and households are polarising into non-owners and multi-owners, consolidating a dual society.

Assessment: The EFF data do show a rise in both non-owning and multi-owning households. But 'property' includes garages, storage rooms, shops and offices, so multi-ownership is not housing multi-ownership; no alternative drivers (household formation, immigration, ageing/inheritance) are examined; and 'the housing problem is a distribution problem' is a framing that the descriptive data cannot by themselves establish against supply/price explanations.

C2 partial mainempirical descriptive non trivialpp. 4, 6-7

Households living in a home they own fell from 79% to 63.9% in 14 years; tenants rose from 11.9% to 19.2%; landlord households almost tripled from 3.4% to 9.8%.

“los hogares que viven en una vivienda de su propiedad han caído del 79 % al 63,9 %”

Assessment: Tenant and landlord shares are reported consistently. But by the report's own definitions (p. 15) 'propietario' excludes owner-occupiers who also receive rental income, so 63.9% is not the share of households living in their own home. Using footnote 1, owner-occupiers in 2022 are roughly 63.85% + 8.4% ≈ 72%. The headline fall in owner-occupation is therefore overstated.

C3 weak mainempirical descriptive non trivialpp. 4, 8, 14

For the first time, multi-owners are the majority among property holders (51.7% in 2025 vs 46.1% in 2008).

Assessment: True in Catastro holder counts, which count any urban property unit (e.g. a flat plus a parking space = 2). In the report's own household data (EFF), multi-owners are 37.1/(40.9+37.1) = 47.6% of owning households in 2022, i.e. still a minority. The 'majority' conclusion depends on the source and on counting non-residential units.

C4 weak mainempirical causal non trivialpp. 4, 9, 14

Growth of the property stock has mainly benefited those who already held property; the larger a household's initial portfolio, the faster it grew.

“cuanto mayor era el patrimonio inmobiliario inicial de un hogar, más rápido ha crecido en los últimos años”

Assessment: The data compare the size of portfolio-size categories at two dates, not the trajectories of the same households. Growth in the '6-10 properties' category can arise from households moving up from smaller bands (or inheriting), so the statement about households' 'initial' portfolios is an ecological inference the cross-sections cannot support. The EFF panel component could test it but is not used. Note also that >10 holders grew less (+35.3%) than 6-10 (+51.6%), so the gradient is not monotonic.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Descriptive comparison of two years (2008 vs 2022 in EFF; 2008 vs 2025 in Catastro), no modelling or uncertainty measures.

Scope: Spain, 2008-2025.

Methods

  • Weighted shares of households by tenure and number of properties
  • Percentage change of holder counts by portfolio band
  • Totals of properties by band

Transparency notes

Definitions of tenure profiles and property are given in a short note; no sampling errors/confidence intervals for EFF estimates (important for small groups like >10 properties); no breakdown of residential vs non-residential units; whether Catastro holders include legal persons is not stated.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Catastro and EFF property volumes differ due to methodology and category definitions.
  • Footnote 1: excluding landlord households who rent their own residence lowers the landlord share from 9.8% to 8.4%.

Issues (6)

I1 major measurement 'Property' includes non-residential unitsp. 15

Per the methodological note, EFF 'inmuebles' include garages, warehouses, shops, offices and hotels; Catastro urban units likewise include parking spaces and storage rooms. A household owning its flat and a garage is a 'multi-owner'. The report nonetheless interprets results as housing concentration ('el problema de la vivienda'). No residential-only breakdown is shown.

Impact on conclusions: Multi-ownership levels and possibly trends are not interpretable as housing concentration without a dwellings-only analysis.

Affects: C1C3C5

I2 major internal inconsistency Headline tenure figure contradicts the report's own definitionspp. 4, 6, 15

The executive summary says households living in their own home fell from 79% to 63.9%, but 'propietario' excludes owner-occupiers who receive rent (classified as 'caseros'). With footnote 1, owner-occupancy in 2022 is ≈72%. Part of the fall in 'owners' is owners becoming landlords, not losing ownership.

Impact on conclusions: Overstates the decline of owner-occupation by roughly 8 points.

Affects: C2

I3 moderate logic Ecological inference about household trajectoriespp. 9, 14

Changes in the size of portfolio bands between two cross-sections are read as faster growth for households with larger initial portfolios. Band membership changes over time; panel data would be needed.

Impact on conclusions: The 'rich get richer' mechanism is plausible but not shown.

Affects: C1C4

I4 moderate missing counterevidence Alternative explanations not examined

Household count grew ~11% (implied 17.3M → 19.1M), with new households (young, immigrant) typically non-owners; ageing and inheritance increase multi-ownership mechanically. Neither demographic composition nor age-specific rates are analysed.

Impact on conclusions: Polarisation may be partly compositional.

Affects: C1C6C7

I5 moderate internal inconsistency Two sources, two periods, mixed in headline claimspp. 4, 8-10, 14

Catastro (holders, 2008-2025) and EFF (households, 2008-2022) are combined in single summary statements (e.g. point 3 mixes Catastro band growth with EFF property counts). EFF-implied growth of household-held properties (+6.2M) is three times the '2 million' Catastro stock growth used in the conclusion. The multi-owner 'majority' holds in Catastro (51.7%) but not in EFF (47.6%).

Impact on conclusions: Readers cannot tell which source supports which number.

Affects: C3C7

I6 minor transparency No sampling uncertainty for survey estimatesp. 10

EFF estimates for small groups (>10 properties, 4-5 properties) are reported to the unit (e.g. 625,883) without confidence intervals.

Impact on conclusions: Spurious precision for small groups.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: A short descriptive policy report based on official microdata. It should be judged on whether headline statements faithfully reflect the indicators, on construct validity (does 'property' mean housing?), on consistency across sources, and on whether descriptive trends are over-interpreted causally.

X6 definition drift major What counts as property and as an owner across the ministry series

Report 2 counts any real-estate unit (garages, shops, offices) as 'inmuebles' and defines 'propietario' excluding owner-occupying landlords (so 63.9% is not owner-occupation). Report 3 of the same series restricts wealth and landlord status to dwellings, and its age-specific 2022 ownership rates (31.8%-84%) imply overall owner-occupation around 72-75%. Report 1 counts cadastral references (possibly including non-dwelling units).

Implication: Headline figures of Report 2 (owner decline to 63.9%, multi-owner majority) are not comparable with Reports 1 and 3 and overstate housing concentration.
