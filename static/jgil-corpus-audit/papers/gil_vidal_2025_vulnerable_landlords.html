Argues that the 'mom-and-pop' and 'vulnerable landlord' figures invoked against rent control in Spain are hegemonic myths, using discourse examples, Barcelona ownership-concentration data and LCS 2023 income/poverty microdata.

Thesis (C1) The 'mom-and-pop' and 'vulnerable landlord' figures are myths: Spanish landlords are a small, high-income minority, typically multi-property, and the myths serve to obscure the rental structure and preserve capitalist class interests (Gramscian hegemony). partial

The 'vulnerable landlord' myth is convincingly refuted with LCS 2023 data: landlord households are a ~9% minority with far higher incomes and very low poverty rates, even under simulated rent cuts. The 'mom-and-pop' critique is valid in its dwelling-share form for Barcelona but misstated as 'most landlords are multi-property' and extrapolated to Spain. Discourse evidence is illustrative and intent is inferred rather than shown.

Summary

Framed through Gramscian hegemony, the paper documents how politicians (PSOE, Junts, PP, Cs, CC, ERC), the government (85% of leases owned by individuals), the Bank of Spain (92%), media and even some activists portray landlords as small, ordinary and economically vulnerable. It 'debunks' this in two parts. Mom-and-pop myth: landlords are only 3-10% of households (CIS, LCS, EFF, IRPF); official statistics lump all non-corporate landlords as 'particulares'; and Barcelona OMHB data show single-dwelling landlords are 79% of landlords but own only 38.7% of rented dwellings, while 1.2% (15+ dwellings) own 24.1%. Vulnerable-landlord myth (LCS 2023): landlord households' median disposable income €45,977 vs tenants €22,851 and non-landlord owners €30,433; even without rental income, landlords earn 67% more than tenants; 29.45% 'high-rent' landlords receive 61.49% of rents; only 6.47% of landlord households are at risk of poverty (0.6% of all households), rising to 7.63% with a 20% rent cut. The authors conclude the myths conceal a polarised rental structure to preserve class interests and argue for rent regulation.

Research questions

  1. How do 'mom-and-pop' and 'vulnerable landlord' discourses misrepresent the Spanish rental structure?
  2. What does available statistical evidence show about landlords' prevalence, concentration and socioeconomic position?
  3. What role do these myths play in the politics of rent control?

Stated contribution

Exposes contradictions between landlord myths and the actual rentier structure and situates the myths within hegemony-building strategies.

C1 partial thesisinterpretive contestedpp. 1, 15-16

The 'mom-and-pop' and 'vulnerable landlord' figures are myths: Spanish landlords are a small, high-income minority, typically multi-property, and the myths serve to obscure the rental structure and preserve capitalist class interests (Gramscian hegemony).

Assessment: The empirical debunking of vulnerability is strong (income and poverty data). The 'typically multi-property' part is contradicted by the paper's own Barcelona table (79% of landlords have one dwelling — the majority of dwellings, not landlords, belong to multi-owners) and rests on a single city. The functional/intentional claim (myths exist to preserve class interests) is an interpretation; the discourse sample is selected, not systematic, and intent is inferred from content.

Landlords are not 'everypersons': they represent between 3% and 10% of Spanish households.

Assessment: Three independent high-quality sources converge on ~9-10%; CIS lower. Consistent with other corpus reports (9.3-9.8%).

C3 adequate maininterpretive non trivialpp. 9-11

Official statistics and surveys mislead by classifying all non-corporate landlords as 'particulares' regardless of portfolio size (CIS 81.5%, EMVS 88.8%, government 85%, Bank of Spain 92%).

Assessment: Question designs are accurately described and the category indeed conflates portfolio sizes. However, statements like the Bank of Spain's '92% owned by private individuals' are factually about legal form, not claims that owners are small; labelling them 'myths' conflates an accurate statistic with the inference others draw from it.

In Barcelona, single-dwelling landlords are 79% of landlords but own only 38.7% of rented dwellings; landlords with 2+ own 60.3%, and 1.2% (15+ dwellings) own 24.1%.

C8 partial mainempirical causal non trivialpp. 14-15, Table 2

Rent income is very unequally distributed among landlords (29.45% receive 61.49%); low-rent landlords (41.53%) would be unlikely to be affected by the 2023 index-based rent regulation, which would mainly hit high-rent landlords.

Assessment: Table 2 is internally consistent. Evidence & data

Design

Mixed. Critical discourse illustration combined with secondary and microdata descriptive statistics.

Scope: Spain (Barcelona for concentration), 2020-2024.

Methods

  • Illustrative discourse analysis (no systematic sampling)
  • Descriptive statistics by tenure
  • Simple static simulation of rent cuts on poverty rates

Transparency notes

Quote selection criteria not stated; LCS landlord definition (housing vs any property/land rent) not stated; OMHB table scope (natural persons only or all landlords) not explicit; income-quartile thresholds not fully explained.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Scarcity of Spanish data on rental ownership structure and landlord profiles.
  • Barcelona data only cover dwellings within the municipality (likely understating concentration).
  • CIS is 'a less rigorous source'.

Issues (8)

I1 major internal inconsistency 'Majority are multi-property landlords' contradicted by own table; Barcelona extrapolated to Spainpp. 9, 11-12, 16

The text states non-corporate landlords are mostly multi-property, yet Table 1 shows 79% own one dwelling; what is true is that 60.3% of dwellings belong to multi-owners. The conclusion then asserts this holds for 'Spain's rental units' using only Barcelona data.

Impact on conclusions: The concentration argument survives in its dwelling-share form, for Barcelona.

Affects: C1C5

I2 moderate sampling Selective discourse samplepp. 5-7

Quotes are chosen to illustrate the myth; no corpus, selection rule, frequency count or counter-examples (e.g. politicians distinguishing small landlords from large holders — Rufián's quote actually does this).

Impact on conclusions: Shows the myth exists, not how dominant it is.

Affects: C1

I3 moderate logic Functionalist inference of intentpp. 9-10, 15-16

Discourses are said to be deployed 'deliberately' and 'with the aim of' preserving class interests; intent is inferred from content and beneficiaries. The paper concedes that activists and statistical offices also use the same categories, which weakens a purely interest-driven explanation.

Impact on conclusions: The hegemony reading is plausible but not demonstrated.

Affects: C1C10

I7 moderate measurement Rent-group classification cannot identify who rent control affectspp. 14-15

Groups are based on a landlord's total annual rent received, which conflates rent per dwelling, number of dwellings and months let; index-based control depends on each dwelling's rent relative to the local index.

Impact on conclusions: The 'low-rent landlords unaffected' inference is unsupported.

Affects: C8

I4 minor conceptual Accurate statistics labelled as mythspp. 7, 12

'92% of rented housing is owned by private individuals' is a statement about legal form that the authors do not dispute; the myth lies in equating individuals with small/vulnerable owners. The text sometimes blurs the two (e.g. 'all assert that 85%-95% of the rental market is controlled by private individuals who are small-scale landlords').

Impact on conclusions: Rhetorical overreach.

Affects: C3

I5 minor transparency Scope of Barcelona table unclearp. 11

It is not explicit whether Table 1 covers natural persons only (as the text implies) or all landlords including companies; this matters for the >15 group (24.1% of dwellings).

Impact on conclusions: Could change the interpretation of top concentration.

Affects: C4

I6 minor transparency LCS landlord definition not givenp. 8

The paper does not state whether 'landlord households' in LCS are identified by housing rental income specifically or by any property/land rental income.

Impact on conclusions: Likely small given convergence with other sources.

Affects: C6

I8 minor internal inconsistency Inconsistent poverty figurespp. 15-16

Tenant at-risk-of-poverty rate is 30.16% in the results and 36.28% in the conclusions; landlord rate 6.47% vs 6.48%. The 36.28% figure coincides with the LCS-2021 estimate (36.3%) published in Vidal, Gil & Martínez (2024), suggesting the conclusion was not updated after switching to LCS 2023.

Impact on conclusions: Does not alter the contrast.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: A critical political-economy paper combining illustrative discourse analysis with descriptive statistics. Standard: accuracy and reconciliation of figures, whether the data used actually test the myths as stated, representativeness of discourse evidence, and restraint in attributing intent.
