Using the AEAT/IEF IRPF Household Panel (2016, 2023), the report finds that 52.8% of privately let dwellings belong to landlords with 2+ rented dwellings, ~61% of the whole market once companies and public housing are added, and that multi-landlords grew faster (2016-2023).

Thesis (C1) The Spanish rental market is dominated not by the small single-dwelling landlord but by multi-landlords, and increasingly operates under a logic of concentration. partial

The report credibly shows that a slight majority of declared private rentals belong to landlords with more than one let dwelling, and that this share rose slightly between 2016 and 2023. Its rhetorical framing outpaces the evidence: the counting rule based on cadastral references is ambiguous, the ~60% figure includes public housing, the city comparisons describe where landlords live rather than where dwellings are, and the trend is modest.

Summary

Against the image of the small single-dwelling landlord, the report estimates from IRPF Household Panel microdata (2023) that 52.8% of dwellings let by individuals belong to landlords with two or more rented dwellings. Adding ~223,552 dwellings owned by legal persons (8% of the private market, inferred from a Banco de España estimate) and ~300,000 public/social dwellings, single-dwelling landlords hold ~39.2% of the rental stock and 'multi-landlords' (including legal persons and public entities) ~60.8%. Among eight large cities (selected as top-10 by rent/m2), the multi-landlord share ranges from 64.9% (Las Palmas) to 46.8% (Sevilla). Between 2016 and 2023, dwellings let by individuals rose from 1.90M to 2.57M; single-dwelling landlords' stock grew 30.4% vs 39.9% for multi-landlords. The report concludes that the market increasingly operates under a logic of concentration, a trend expected to continue. A methodological note explains that multi-landlord status is determined by counting unique cadastral references linked to each landlord household and that city results reflect the landlord's fiscal residence, not the location of dwellings.

Research questions

  1. What share of the rental housing stock belongs to single-dwelling vs multi-dwelling landlords?
  2. How does this vary across large cities?
  3. How did the composition evolve between 2016 and 2023?

Stated contribution

First approximation (in the absence of an official register) of rental-market ownership structure in Spain using tax microdata.

How the argument is built

1) Private landlords: 52.8% multi; city breakdown. 2) Whole market incl. legal persons and public housing: ~60% multi. 3) 2016-2023 evolution. Synthesis. Methodological note.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Descriptive analysis of administrative tax-panel microdata, supplemented with an external estimate for corporate and public stock.

Scope: Spain (common fiscal territory), 2016-2023; eight cities.

Methods

  • Weighted counts of rented dwellings by landlord portfolio size
  • Combination with external estimates of corporate and public stock
  • Two-point change 2016-2023
  • City breakdown by landlord fiscal residence

Transparency notes

Variable names and sample restrictions are given, which is good practice. Not reported: number of landlord households, whether cadastral references are restricted to rented dwellings (vs garages/storage or the landlord's own home), treatment of co-owned dwellings, sampling errors.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Corporate/public estimates are an approximation 'subject to some margin of error'.
  • City results reflect fiscal residence of landlords, not location of dwellings.
  • Panel starts in 2016.
  • Excludes foral territories.

Issues (6)

I1 major measurement Ambiguous counting rule for 'multi-landlord'p. 14

Multi-landlord status is 'determined by aggregating and counting unique cadastral references linked to each landlord household in the Panel's real-estate module'. Garages, storage rooms and the landlord's own residence each have distinct cadastral references. Unless counting is restricted to dwellings actually let (not stated), landlords letting one flat could be classified as multi-landlords. Co-owned dwellings may be counted once per co-owning household.

Impact on conclusions: Could materially inflate the 52.8% headline; direction and size unknown from the report.

Affects: C1C2

I5 major internal inconsistency City results describe landlords' residence, not city marketspp. 4, 7-8, 14

The methodological note states that municipal figures reflect the portfolios of landlords who file taxes in the city, wherever their dwellings are. The executive summary and section 1 nevertheless describe them as the share of each city's rental market controlled by multi-landlords and infer a link with local price pressure.

Impact on conclusions: City comparisons cannot support claims about local markets.

Affects: C4C5

I2 moderate conceptual Public housing counted as 'multiarrendadores'pp. 9-10, 13

The ~60% figure groups private multi-landlords, companies and ~300,000 public/social dwellings, and the synthesis describes the whole as multi-landlords concentrating the market. Public landlords are not the object of the 'concentration' critique. Excluding them, single landlords hold 43.4% and private multi + corporate 56.6%.

Impact on conclusions: The 60/40 headline mixes conceptually different actors.

Affects: C1C3

I3 moderate data quality Coverage limited to declared rentalspp. 6, 9-10

Tax data capture only declared residential lettings in the common fiscal territory. The report's own figure of 20.2% tenant households (with the companion report's ~19.1M households) implies ~3.9M renting households vs the 3.09M dwellings counted. Who owns the undeclared segment is unknown, so shares may be biased.

Impact on conclusions: Shares apply to the declared market only.

Affects: C2C3

I4 minor overgeneralization Modest change read as structural concentration; ecological inferencepp. 11-13

The single-landlord share moves from ~49.0% to 47.2% in seven years. Growth by category is read as growth by incumbents with several dwellings, though landlords change category. Formalisation of previously undeclared lettings could also differ by landlord type.

Impact on conclusions: Direction credible, magnitude small; the 'clear trend' language is strong.

Affects: C1C6C7

I6 minor presentation Landlord vs dwelling share confusionpp. 7-8

Section 1 text says '64.9% of landlords are multi-landlords' while the chart and summary report '% of rented dwellings'. These are different quantities (the landlord share is necessarily lower).

Impact on conclusions: Confusing; probably a wording error.

Links to the rest of the corpus

X6 definition drift major What counts as property and as an owner across the ministry series

Report 2 counts any real-estate unit (garages, shops, offices) as 'inmuebles' and defines 'propietario' excluding owner-occupying landlords (so 63.9% is not owner-occupation). Report 3 of the same series restricts wealth and landlord status to dwellings, and its age-specific 2022 ownership rates (31.8%-84%) imply overall owner-occupation around 72-75%. Report 1 counts cadastral references (possibly including non-dwelling units).

Implication: Headline figures of Report 2 (owner decline to 63.9%, multi-owner majority) are not comparable with Reports 1 and 3 and overstate housing concentration.

X7 corroboration positive Landlord prevalence converges at ~9-10% of households

LCS 9.32% / 9% (2021), EFF 9.87% / 9.79% (2022), IRPF 9.04% / 9.3% all agree; CIS gives 3.2-3.9% (narrower question). EFF 2020 at 7% (Delclós et al.) is the outlier.

Implication: One of the most robust findings of the corpus.

X9 corroboration positive Multi-landlord share of dwellings: Barcelona vs Spain

Barcelona deposit-register data (OMHB) give 60.3% of privately let dwellings owned by multi-property landlords; the IRPF panel gives 60.8% for Barcelona-resident landlords and 52.8% nationally. Gil, Vidal & Martínez (2025) had extrapolated the Barcelona figure to 'the majority of Spain's rental units' before national data existed; Report 1 later supports a (narrower) national majority.

Implication: Independent sources converge; note the 2025 paper's separate error of saying most landlords (not dwellings) are multi-property.
