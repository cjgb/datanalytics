InsideAirbnb data for Madrid show a supply dominated by entire apartments, 'commercial' (high-availability) listings and multi-listing hosts, concentrated in the Central District — i.e. professionalised 'airbnbisation' rather than a sharing economy.

Thesis (C1) Airbnb in Madrid is dominated by professional actors specialised in renting entire apartments as short-term rentals, mainly in the Central District, whose activity does not comply with sharing-economy principles. adequate

The cross-sectional picture of professionalised, centrally concentrated Airbnb supply in Madrid is robust: nearly every reported share reconciles with the others. Weaknesses are the retrospective growth series (survivorship and back-dating bias), the blanket equation of multi-listing with professional non-resident hosting, an arithmetic slip ('nearly 10 points'), and an impact claim in the abstract that the paper itself concedes has not been studied.

Summary

Opening with 'Claudia' — the pseudonym of Friendly Rentals (Wyndham) with 211 Madrid listings — the paper argues that Madrid's city centre undergoes 'airbnbisation' driven by professional short-term-rental operators. Using one InsideAirbnb scrape (18,332 active listings; date not stated) it analyses spatial distribution, accommodation type, p2p vs commercial listings (183-day availability rule), multi-listing hosts and listing accumulation, plus a reconstructed growth series based on host registration dates and a comparison with April 2017 data. The Central District holds 47.78% of listings; entire apartments are 63% citywide and 76.3% in the centre; commercial listings 75.7% (80.07% centre); multi-listing hosts control 59.8% of listings (68.85% centre); hosts with 6+ listings (4.3% of hosts) control 32.1% of listings, and their share grew since 2017. The paper concludes the model resembles traditional accommodation, harms the city's economic sustainability, and recommends banning commercial listings plus a territorial cap.

Research questions

  1. Is Madrid's Airbnb supply dominated by entire apartments, commercial listings and multi-listing hosts?
  2. Is the supply disproportionately concentrated in the Central District, with over-representation of professional listings there?
  3. Has Airbnb's growth reduced or reinforced professionalisation?

Stated contribution

An in-depth temporal and territorial examination of professionalisation, concentration and 'monopolisation' of Airbnb in Madrid.

How the argument is built

Sharing-economy principle (idle capacity) → Airbnb's self-narrative ('Belong Anywhere') → literature on touristification and rent gaps → five indicators → results by district and over time → principles under which sharing-economy benefits could materialise → policy recommendation.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Descriptive cross-sectional analysis of one InsideAirbnb snapshot with a retrospective growth reconstruction and a comparison with an April 2017 snapshot from earlier work.

Scope: Madrid, 2010s to c. 2019.

Methods

  • Shares by district, accommodation type, p2p/commercial rule, host listing-count band
  • Retrospective growth series using host registration date
  • Comparison of market shares with April 2017

Transparency notes

Scrape date is never stated; no table of counts by band (only shares and graphs); growth method is described and its survivorship limitation footnoted.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Growth series counts only listings active at scrape time and uses host registration date (fn. 4).
  • p2p rule is described as the most lenient possible.

Issues (6)

I6 major internal inconsistency Impact claim in abstract contradicted by conclusionp. 1; p. 16

The abstract concludes the model 'generates negative impacts on the economic sustainability of the city and its inhabitants', but no impact is measured and the conclusion states that a study of impacts on rents, supply and displacement 'would be necessary'.

Impact on conclusions: The impact conclusion should be treated as a hypothesis.

Affects: C10

I2 moderate measurement Availability-based classificationp. 6

Same 182-day calendar-availability rule as in Gil (2021): booked nights are not 'available', calendar defaults vary, so the rule is a noisy proxy for residential use. No sensitivity analysis.

Impact on conclusions: Magnitude uncertain; the citywide direction is plausible.

Affects: C1C4

I3 moderate conceptual Multi-listing ≠ professional / non-resident in all casespp. 6, 13

The paper asserts that all multi-listing hosts are professionals not hosting in their own residence, yet 44.5% of listings in the 2-5 band are private rooms. Hosts with several rooms in their own home would be misclassified.

Impact on conclusions: Concentration findings stand; 'professional' label overstated for the 2-5 band.

Affects: C1C5C6

I4 moderate measurement Retrospective growth series biasedp. 5, fn. 4; p. 13

Growth is reconstructed from host registration dates for listings active at scrape time (acknowledged in fn. 4). This omits exited listings and back-dates listings added later by long-standing hosts, and it classifies past listings using current availability. The 2017-vs-now comparison relies on an earlier paper's scrape whose comparability is not documented.

Impact on conclusions: Temporal claims are weaker than the cross-sectional ones.

Affects: C7C8

I1 minor arithmetic Misstated gap ('nearly 10 points')p. 9

Central commercial share 80.07% vs city average 75.7% is a 4.4-point gap, not 'nearly 10 points' (the gap to the rest of the city is 8.4).

Impact on conclusions: Presentational; direction unchanged.

Affects: C4

I5 minor transparency Scrape date not reported; '>50%' in conclusionpp. 7, 13

The date of the main dataset is never given. The conclusion says over 50% of supply 'has been' in the Central District, while the current figure is 47.78%.

Impact on conclusions: Minor.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: Descriptive quantitative analysis of scraped platform data. Judged on reconciliation of reported figures, validity of proxies for 'professional' and 'commercial', soundness of the temporal reconstruction, and whether impact conclusions are matched by impact evidence.

Strengths

  • Exceptional internal numerical consistency across districts and host bands.
  • Clear, reusable indicator framework.
  • Genuine two-point comparison with April 2017 data.
  • Acknowledges survivorship limitation of the growth series.

Weaknesses

  • Scrape date missing.
  • Growth series methodologically weak.
  • 'Professional' label applied to all multi-listing hosts.
  • Impact on city sustainability asserted but not studied.
