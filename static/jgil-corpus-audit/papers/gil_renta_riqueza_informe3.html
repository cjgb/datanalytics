Using EFF microdata (2002-2022), the report shows large income and wealth gaps between tenants, owner-occupiers and landlords, rising concentration of housing wealth, and a collapse of youth homeownership, and argues that tenure divides more than age.

Thesis (C1) Housing has become the main axis of reproduction of economic inequality in Spain: access to ownership, accumulation of housing wealth and rental income reinforce each other. partial

The strongest of the three ministry reports: descriptive income and wealth gaps by tenure, the age-wealth gradient and the rise in housing-wealth concentration are well estimated and internally consistent. The interpretive layer overreaches — the comparison showing tenure 'divides more' than age is largely definitional, causal language about young people's wealth is unsupported, and 'main axis of inequality' is asserted without comparison.

Summary

Median household income (2022): tenants €21,335, owner-occupiers €32,120, single-dwelling landlords €50,959, multi-landlords €80,375 (rising to €213,120 for 4+ let dwellings). Median net wealth: tenants €2,217, owners €193,919, landlords €407,975, multi-landlords €996,826 (184x and 450x tenants). By age, income differences are moderate (€19,651-34,200) while wealth rises from €20,069 (<35) to €223,960 (65-74). Homeownership among under-35 households fell from 69.3% (2011) to 31.8% (2022). Housing wealth concentration increased: top 10% share 34.5% → 41.9% (2002-2022), Gini 0.50 (2005) → 0.591 (2022). The report concludes that the rental market transfers income from poorer tenants to richer landlords, that tenure rather than age is the central axis of inequality ('young people have less wealth mainly because they are tenants'), and that housing has become the main axis of reproduction of economic inequality. The methodological note describes weights, multiple imputation, a strict housing-only landlord and wealth definition, and CPI deflation.

Research questions

  1. How do income and wealth differ between tenants, owner-occupiers and landlords (by number of let dwellings)?
  2. How do age-group differences compare with tenure-group differences?
  3. How has concentration of housing wealth evolved 2002-2022?

Stated contribution

Updated descriptive picture of housing-related income and wealth inequality in Spain from EFF microdata.

The rental market functions as a channel transferring income from lower-income tenants to higher-income landlords.

Assessment: On average, rent flows from poorer to richer households given the group medians, and the conclusion hedges ('estaría funcionando'). C7 weak mainempirical causal contestedpp. 5, 18-21, 29

Position in the housing market divides more than generation: young people have less wealth not because they are young but mainly because they are tenants; tenure, not age, is the central axis of inequality.

“las personas jóvenes no tienen menos patrimonio porque sean jóvenes, sino, principalmente, porque son inquilinas”

Assessment: The comparison is of the range of group medians across tenure categories vs across age bands. Tenure categories are defined by property ownership — the main component of household wealth — so they separate wealth almost by construction; the extreme 'multi-landlord' group is a small fraction of households while age bands are broad. A valid comparison would decompose inequality (e.g. between-group Theil/variance shares) or estimate wealth with both age and tenure jointly. The causal claim ('because they are tenants') also reverses a plausible direction: low wealth prevents buying.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Descriptive weighted medians and concentration measures from repeated cross-sections of a household wealth survey.

Scope: Spain, 2002-2022.

Methods

  • Weighted medians by tenure, landlord portfolio size and age
  • Top-share and Gini of housing wealth over time
  • Lorenz curves

Transparency notes

Methodological note is detailed (question numbers, weights, imputation, deflation). Not reported: sample sizes per group (e.g. multi-landlords with 4+ dwellings), confidence intervals, age definition (household head or reference person), and the shares of households in each tenure group.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • EFF questionnaire only itemises three additional properties; '4+' is an open category.
  • 2024 microdata not yet available.

Issues (6)

I2 major logic Circular tenure-vs-age comparisonpp. 18-21, 29

Tenure groups are defined by property ownership, which is most of household wealth, so they differ in wealth largely by construction; comparing ranges of medians across groups of very different sizes (small multi-landlord group vs broad age bands) is not a test of 'which divides more'. Causality from tenure to wealth is asserted although low wealth also causes tenancy.

Impact on conclusions: The 'tenure not age' conclusion is not established by the analysis shown.

Affects: C4C5C7

I1 moderate overgeneralization 'Main axis of inequality' not tested against alternativespp. 5, 30

No comparison with other determinants (education, employment, financial wealth, inheritance). The executive summary says 'el principal eje', the conclusion 'uno de los principales ejes'.

Impact on conclusions: Headline should read 'a major axis'.

Affects: C1

I4 moderate transparency No sample sizes or uncertainty

Medians for small groups (multi-landlords with 3 or 4+ dwellings; under-35 household heads per wave) are reported without n or confidence intervals, despite the EFF providing the means to compute them.

Impact on conclusions: Precision of extreme-group figures and of the youth-ownership collapse unknown.

Affects: C2C6

I6 moderate missing counterevidence Compositional change in young households ignoredpp. 14-17

Delayed emancipation changes who appears as an under-35 household; selection into household formation and immigration could account for part of the ownership drop.

Impact on conclusions: The 'exclusion' interpretation may overstate the change for the cohort as a whole.

Affects: C6

I3 minor causal inference Transfer mechanism inferred from group medianspp. 8-9

Rent flows are not traced between specific tenants and landlords; some tenants rent from corporate/public landlords; landlords' income includes rent received.

Impact on conclusions: Direction plausible; the statement is appropriately hedged in the conclusion.

Affects: C3

I5 minor presentation Selective base yearspp. 4, 25-26

Top-share change uses 2002, Gini change uses 2005 (series minimum). From 2002, the Gini rise is +15% not +18%.

Impact on conclusions: Small overstatement.

Links to the rest of the corpus

X6 definition drift major What counts as property and as an owner across the ministry series

Report 2 counts any real-estate unit (garages, shops, offices) as 'inmuebles' and defines 'propietario' excluding owner-occupying landlords (so 63.9% is not owner-occupation). Report 3 of the same series restricts wealth and landlord status to dwellings, and its age-specific 2022 ownership rates (31.8%-84%) imply overall owner-occupation around 72-75%. Report 1 counts cadastral references (possibly including non-dwelling units).

Implication: Headline figures of Report 2 (owner decline to 63.9%, multi-owner majority) are not comparable with Reports 1 and 3 and overstate housing concentration.

X8 corroboration positive Landlords' income advantage is consistent across sources and years

Median incomes: LCS 2021 landlords €40,293 vs tenants €18,457; LCS 2023 €45,977 vs €22,851; EFF 2022 single landlords €50,959, multi-landlords €80,375, tenants €21,335. Ratios of ~2x (LCS) and 2.4-3.8x (EFF) are consistent with definitional differences.
