Using a July-2019 InsideAirbnb snapshot, the paper argues that Airbnb in Valencia follows a 'touristifying' rather than 'redistributive' model: listings are mostly commercial, concentrated in tourist districts and among multi-listing hosts, and income is highly concentrated.

Thesis (C1) Airbnb in Valencia has produced a 'touristifying' rather than a 'redistributive' hosting model. adequate

The descriptive core — Valencia's Airbnb supply in 2019 was predominantly entire-home, high-availability, spatially concentrated and controlled disproportionately by multi-listing hosts — is well supported and survives the several arithmetic slips identified (none changes a conclusion). The rent-gap magnitude is an assumption-heavy estimate, and the housing-market effects (supply loss, rent increases, displacement) are inferred from literature and exposure ratios rather than shown for Valencia.

Summary

The paper contrasts two ideal-typical hosting models: a 'redistributive' one (Airbnb's advertised peer-to-peer model: residents sharing their underused homes, dispersed across the city) and a 'touristifying' one (professional multi-hosts operating dedicated units in tourist areas). Eight indicators are computed for Valencia from InsideAirbnb (31 July 2019; 7,203 active short-term listings), AirDNA district occupancy rates, Idealista rents and INE housing stock: listing type (p2p vs commercial by >182 days of calendar availability), accommodation type, listing concentration among hosts, district distribution, a district rent gap (STR minus long-term rent), share of the rental stock, guest capacity relative to residents, and income distribution. 76% of listings and ~89% of offered nights are classified as commercial; 58.5% of listings belong to multi-listing hosts; two tourist districts hold 38.7% of listings; converting a home to STR yields an estimated €500-1,200 more per month; entire-home listings equal 10.3% of the rental stock (39.6% in Ciutat Vella); the top 10% of hosts earn 52.8% of revenue. The author concludes Valencia exhibits the touristifying model, which reduces rental supply, raises rents and displaces residents, and calls for regulation.

Research questions

  1. What type of hosting supply does Airbnb produce in Valencia?
  2. What is the relationship between Airbnb and the residential housing market?
  3. To what extent does Airbnb redistribute the benefits of tourism?

Stated contribution

A city-level diagnostic framework (eight variables) to classify whether Airbnb in a city follows a redistributive or touristifying model, applied to Valencia.

How the argument is built

Intro: Airbnb's four advertised benefits. Literature: sharing economy requires idle assets; conversion of homes, rent-gap theory, professionalisation and spatial concentration. Method: eight variables. Results: 4.1-4.8. Conclusion: touristifying model, with claimed effects on rental supply, rents, neighbourhoods and displacement; COVID as regulatory window.

C1 adequate thesisinterpretive non trivialp. 21

Airbnb in Valencia has produced a 'touristifying' rather than a 'redistributive' hosting model.

Assessment: Given the paper's own ideal types, the descriptive indicators (commercial share, host concentration, spatial concentration, revenue concentration) consistently point the same way, and the conclusion is robust to the arithmetic slips found. The classification is, however, partly built into the operational thresholds.

C2 adequate mainempirical descriptive non trivialpp. 9-11

About three quarters of listings (75.8%) and close to nine tenths of offered nights are commercial rather than peer-to-peer.

Assessment: Counts are internally consistent (1,745 + 5,458 = 7,203). Nights shares are misreported in one place (11.8%/88.2% instead of 10.87%/89.13%) but the direction is unaffected. The 182-day availability threshold is a proxy whose error is not symmetric: booked nights are excluded from availability, so very busy listings may be classified as p2p (conservative), while hosts who leave calendars open by default may be classified commercial (non-conservative).

Assessment: Band counts reconcile exactly to 7,203 listings and 4,167 hosts. C6 partial mainempirical comparative common knowledgepp. 8-9, 15-16

Converting a home to a tourist flat yields on average €500-1,200 more per month than long-term letting, and over €1,000/month in tourist districts; this is a conservative estimate.

Assessment: Direction is plausible and consistent with the literature, but the estimate relies on strong assumptions: a uniform 70 m2 dwelling, asking (not contract) rents, list prices, a district-level AirDNA occupancy applied to every listing, and only a 20% cost deduction (no platform fees, taxes, furnishing, licensing, vacancy risk). Some assumptions are conservative, others are not, so the 'conservative' label is not demonstrated. Which listings enter the district mean is not stated.

Evidence & data

Design

Quantitative. Cross-sectional descriptive analysis of a single platform snapshot, combined with district-level secondary data; no counterfactual or time series.

Scope: Valencia city, July 2019.

Methods

  • Rule-based classification of listings (p2p if calendar availability < 183 days)
  • Host concentration by listing-count bands
  • District shares and ratios
  • Rent-gap estimate: price x available days x district occupancy x 0.8 vs 70 m2 x district rent/m2
  • Revenue concentration by host deciles

Transparency notes

Variables and assumptions are described in prose; no tables of district-level values beyond charts; data year of INE stock, AirDNA period and exact rent-gap formula aggregation (mean over which listings?) not specified. Code not provided.

Limitations acknowledged by the authors

  • Availability-based classification is described as a maximally lenient interpretation of 'underused home'.
  • 70 m2 assumption and 20% management cost described as conservative.
  • Exclusion of rooms from rental-stock pressure described as conservative.
  • Guest capacity assumes 100% occupancy.

Issues (7)

I6 major causal inference Causal effects on rents and displacement asserted without Valencia datapp. 17-18, 21

The conclusion lists reduced rental supply, rent pressure, neighbourhood transformation and displacement as effects of the model, but the paper presents no rent trends, eviction/displacement or conversion data for Valencia; these are inferred from literature on other cities.

Impact on conclusions: These conclusions should be read as hypotheses consistent with the indicators.

Affects: C8C9

I2 moderate measurement Calendar availability used as proxy for residential usepp. 6-7, 11

The p2p/commercial split and the 'cannot be habitual residence' inference rest on forward calendar availability, which excludes already-booked nights and reflects host calendar settings rather than actual use. Bias direction is ambiguous, so the 'most lenient possible' characterisation is not established. No sensitivity analysis (e.g. 90- or 120-day thresholds, or reviews-based activity).

Impact on conclusions: Magnitudes uncertain; direction likely robust given the size of the gap.

Affects: C1C2C3

I4 moderate measurement Rent-gap estimate rests on strong, partly non-conservative assumptionspp. 8-9, 15-16

Uniform 70 m2 size, asking rents from Idealista, list prices, district-average occupancy applied uniformly, 20% cost deduction, no platform fees/taxes/licence/furnishing costs. The author argues all choices are conservative, but list prices and omitted costs bias upward. No sensitivity ranges are reported and the averaging unit is unclear.

Impact on conclusions: Existence of a positive gap is credible; the €500-1,200 magnitude is uncertain.

Affects: C6

I5 moderate causal inference Stock ratio interpreted as supply withdrawalp. 17

Entire-home listings are divided by the (undated) INE rental stock. Listings may come from owner-occupied, second or previously vacant homes, may be duplicated, or be in purpose-built tourist apartments; thus the ratio is not a measure of units removed from long-term rental.

Impact on conclusions: Weakens the step from exposure to impact.

Affects: C7C8

I1 minor arithmetic Arithmetic/reporting inconsistenciespp. 6, 10-13

(a) 7,654 − 451 − 46 = 7,157, not the 7,203 analysed (7,203 = 7,654 − 451 only; the 46 long-stay listings appear not to have been removed, or overlap with inactive ones). (b) p2p nights are reported as 11.8% / commercial 88.2% on p. 10, but the reported counts give 10.87% / 89.13% (the conclusion uses 10.87%). (c) p2p listings given as 24.19% on p. 11 vs 24.23% elsewhere. (d) Single-listing hosts 71.91% vs 71.66% computed from counts. (e) Hosts with 6+ listings hold 23.4% of listings, described as 'casi un tercio'. (f) >20-listing hosts: 9.16% not 9.14%.

Impact on conclusions: None on direction of conclusions; indicates limited proofreading.

Affects: C2C4

I3 minor measurement Host accounts ≠ economic actors

Concentration is measured per host account; management companies may use several accounts and single accounts may represent many owners. The bias likely understates concentration among operators but overstates concentration of ownership.

Impact on conclusions: Qualifies interpretation of 'who' controls supply.

Affects: C4

I7 minor measurement Modelled revenue drives the inequality resultpp. 9, 19-20

Revenue is computed from availability, district occupancy and list price, so cross-host dispersion is largely mechanical (number of listings x availability). The step from revenue concentration to 'widening social inequality' needs information on who the hosts are.

Impact on conclusions: Concentration finding stands; inequality interpretation is an extrapolation.

Overall assessment

Evaluation frame: A descriptive quantitative case study using scraped platform data. It should be judged on the validity of its operational definitions, internal arithmetic consistency, appropriate framing of modelled estimates, and whether causal claims about housing effects are backed by data on those effects.

  • Causal housing effects asserted without local outcome data.

X1 contradiction moderate Did pandemic STRs return to the residential market?

Gil (2023) states that during the pandemic 'STRs did not return to the residential market (Gil et al., 2023)' and uses this to argue for housing-policy (not tourism-policy) responses. Gil, Vidal & Yrigoy (2026) cite the same source for STR units being 'temporarily redirected to residential or mid-term segments'. Sequera et al. (2022) document central Madrid long-term listings rising from 1,300 to 4,600 and back to 1,900, and Gil (2021) reported hosts 'directly ending the business and renting in the residential market'. The evidence supports a temporary switch, largely under seasonal contracts; the 2023 paper's unqualified statement understates it.

Implication: The policy argument in Gil (2023) should be qualified: switching occurred but was temporary and often outside long-term residential contracts.

X4 data reuse moderate Same Valencia 2019 scrape, undisclosed change of rent-gap method

Gil (2021) and Gil (2023) analyse the identical 31 July 2019 InsideAirbnb Valencia dataset. Errors propagate (7,654 − 451 − 46 ≠ 7,203; single-host share 71.91% instead of 71.66%). The rent-gap formula changes (availability-based vs occupancy with 15% commission) giving €500-1,200 vs €497-958 per month, without acknowledgement; the 'not habitual residence' threshold shifts from 90/182 days to 60 days.

Implication: Readers cannot tell which estimate is preferred; magnitudes are assumption-driven.

X5 method drift moderate Shifting thresholds for 'commercial' / 'non-residential' listings

Availability >182 days defines commercial listings in Gil (2021) and Gil & Sequera (2020); Gil (2021) also uses >90 days to argue non-residence; Gil (2023) uses >60 days to estimate homes 'extracted from the rental market' and displacement.

Implication: Lower thresholds mechanically raise displacement estimates; sensitivity analysis is never reported.
