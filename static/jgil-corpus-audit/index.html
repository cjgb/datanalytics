Overview

The corpus is a coherent critical political-economy research programme on Spanish housing since 2008. It runs from platform hosting (Airbnb hosts' precarity, 2019) through the professionalisation and 'assetization' of short-term rentals (Valencia, Madrid), the state-led reignition of financialization after 2013, the ownership structure and inequality of the rental market (landlord myths, ministry reports on multi-landlords, concentration and wealth), mid-term rentals as regulatory evasion, and the tenant and anti-eviction movements that contest these processes. The descriptive empirical core — supply concentration on Airbnb, the affluence and small population share of landlords, the rise of multi-ownership, the Barcelona mid-term-rental surge around rent controls — is generally well supported and reproducible from the figures printed in the papers. The recurring weakness is the step from descriptive indicators to causal and structural conclusions (displacement counts, rent effects, 'policy package caused the boom', 'tenure not age divides', 'unions were the key factor'), which is often made without counterfactuals and then carried forward as a premise into later papers. Numerical slips are frequent but almost never change the direction of a finding; a few cross-paper inconsistencies reveal reused analyses not updated.

Themes

T1 Platform hosting, short-term and mid-term rentals

From hosts' lived precarity to the professional, concentrated and assetized structure of Airbnb supply, the pandemic switch, and the mid-term rental turn as regulatory evasion.

Assessment: Strong descriptive evidence of professionalisation and concentration (Madrid especially well reconciled). Housing-market impact claims (rents, supply loss, displacement of 9,047 people) rest on exposure ratios and assumptions. The MTR paper adds hypotheses and regressions, but time-series inference is weak.

Who owns rental housing, how concentrated it is, and the income/wealth gaps between tenants, owners and landlords; the 'mom-and-pop' and 'vulnerable landlord' myths.

X1 contradiction moderate Did pandemic STRs return to the residential market?

Gil (2023) states that during the pandemic 'STRs did not return to the residential market (Gil et al., 2023)' and uses this to argue for housing-policy (not tourism-policy) responses. Gil, Vidal & Yrigoy (2026) cite the same source for STR units being 'temporarily redirected to residential or mid-term segments'. Sequera et al. (2022) document central Madrid long-term listings rising from 1,300 to 4,600 and back to 1,900, and Gil (2021) reported hosts 'directly ending the business and renting in the residential market'. The evidence supports a temporary switch, largely under seasonal contracts; the 2023 paper's unqualified statement understates it.

Implication: The policy argument in Gil (2023) should be qualified: switching occurred but was temporary and often outside long-term residential contracts.

Martínez & Gil (2021, films) report 'on average 80,000 evictions per year between 2009 and 2015' (≈560,000), while Gil & Martínez (2021, state-led) report 409,000 evictions for 2009-2018. The films paper's own footnote gives repossessions of 40,740 (2011) and 64,902 (2015), ~80% homes.

X6 definition drift major What counts as property and as an owner across the ministry series

Report 2 counts any real-estate unit (garages, shops, offices) as 'inmuebles' and defines 'propietario' excluding owner-occupying landlords (so 63.9% is not owner-occupation). Report 3 of the same series restricts wealth and landlord status to dwellings, and its age-specific 2022 ownership rates (31.8%-84%) imply overall owner-occupation around 72-75%. Implication: Headline figures of Report 2 (owner decline to 63.9%, multi-owner majority) are not comparable with Reports 1 and 3 and overstate housing concentration.

Assessment of the research programme

A productive and internally coherent research programme whose descriptive findings are generally sound and often reproducible from the published figures, and whose concepts (STR assetization, policy package, counter-hegemonic legislative strategies, double temporality, landlord myths) are clearly articulated. Its evidential weak point is systematic: causal and structural conclusions are drawn from descriptive or single-case evidence, estimates built on strong assumptions are later cited as findings or minimums, and contested interpretations become premises through self-citation. Work published from 2024 onward (Reports 1 and 3, the mid-term rental paper) shows improved methodological transparency.

Strengths

  • Consistent focus and cumulative development of a clear theoretical agenda.
  • Heavy use of administrative and survey microdata (IRPF panel, EFF, LCS) in later work, with convergent results.
  • Most arithmetic reconciles; Madrid Airbnb and mid-term rental figures are internally exemplary.
  • Explicit positionality in activist/insider research.
  • Willingness to present mixed evidence (e.g. rent-control literature, H3 in the MTR paper).

Recurring weaknesses

  • Causal claims from exposure ratios or before/after sequences without counterfactuals (rent effects, displacement, policy package, union impact).
  • Assumption-driven estimates reframed as minimums (9,047 displaced; ≥27.89% supply loss).
  • Construct validity problems in key indicators (calendar availability, listings as stock, 'inmuebles' including garages, cadastral references, guest capacity as occupancy).
  • Frequent small arithmetic and reporting slips; a few copy-forward errors between papers.
  • Qualitative samples rarely described (interviewee composition, theme frequencies).
  • Insider evaluation of movement outcomes without triangulation.

Evolution over time

2019-2022: theory-led essays and descriptive scraping studies. 2023-2024: conceptual syntheses (assetization, landlord myths, tenants' unions) that lean on earlier findings. 2025-2026: richer administrative data (IRPF, EFF), explicit hypotheses and regressions, and more detailed methodological notes — though inference and construct issues persist.

Recommendations

  • Adopt counterfactual designs for causal claims (difference-in-differences, synthetic controls, interrupted time series).
  • Report ranges and sensitivity analyses for key assumptions; avoid 'minimum' framing for upper-bound scenarios.
  • Harmonise definitions (owner, landlord, property, eviction) across the programme.
  • Publish code and derived tables to allow reconciliation and reuse.
  • Triangulate movement-outcome claims with non-participant sources.
